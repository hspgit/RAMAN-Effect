\begin{abstract}
\emph{Context.} Rapid, culture-free identification of bacteria from Raman
spectra requires short acquisitions, which produce low signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) spectra (SNR $\approx 4.1$ at \sunit{1}).
\emph{Objective.} A 25-layer 1D residual network (ResNet) was shown to
outperform classical classifiers on such data, but it is unclear how much
of that gain comes from the residual connections themselves rather than
depth, downsampling strategy or kernel size.
\emph{Methods.} Using the open-access reference dataset of 60{,}000
spectra from 30 bacterial and yeast isolates, we conduct a controlled
comparison between a 1D ResNet and a lightweight plain 1D CNN without
shortcuts, matching training protocols, data splits, and physical data
augmentations.
\emph{Results.} On 30-isolate identification, the plain CNN reaches
83.9\% test accuracy compared to 79.6\% for the 1D ResNet (and 82.2\%
for the 25-layer reference ResNet). When grouped into eight empiric
antibiotic treatment classes, accuracies reach 96.5\% for the plain CNN
and 94.8\% for the ResNet, with over 80\% of isolate-level errors occurring
between strains within the same treatment category. For binary MRSA versus
MSSA resistance discrimination, the plain CNN achieves an ROC-AUC of 0.941
versus 0.924 for the ResNet. Under simulated SNR degradation down to
$\mathrm{SNR}=1$, average pooling renders the plain CNN more resilient to
stochastic point noise.
\emph{Conclusion.} Residual connections are not required for 1D bacterial
Raman spectra; compact plain CNNs with multi-scale pooling provide equal or
superior diagnostic accuracy with a six-fold reduction in parameter count.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{R}{aman} spectroscopy is a powerful, label-free analytical
technique that enables the characterization of the chemical composition and
molecular structure of materials through their vibrational
properties~\cite{smith2019modern}. The technique is based on the Raman
scattering effect, an inelastic light-scattering phenomenon that occurs when
incident photons interact with molecular bonds and exchange energy with
molecular vibrations. When a sample is illuminated with monochromatic light,
typically from a laser source, the majority of scattered photons undergo
elastic scattering (Rayleigh scattering), retaining the same energy as the
incident photons~\cite{smith2019modern}. A small fraction of photons
undergoes inelastic scattering, resulting in an energy shift that corresponds
to the vibrational modes of the molecules within the sample. The energy shifts
are measured and represented as a Raman spectrum, in which the intensity of
scattered light is plotted as a function of Raman shift (\wn). Because
different molecular bonds and chemical species exhibit characteristic
vibrational signatures, Raman spectra provide a biochemical fingerprint that
can be used to identify and characterize the molecular composition of a
sample~\cite{smith2019modern}.

An important characteristic of Raman spectroscopy is its ability to provide
detailed molecular information without requiring extensive sample
preparation, fluorescent labeling, or chemical
modification~\cite{yi2025surface}. Furthermore, its non-destructive nature
makes it particularly attractive for biological applications, including the
characterization of cells and microorganisms. At the single-cell level, Raman
spectroscopy offers the potential to investigate biochemical heterogeneity by
detecting molecular signatures associated with cellular components such as
proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and carbohydrates. This capability is
particularly valuable when studying cellular populations in which individual
cells may exhibit distinct biochemical characteristics that are not readily
captured through bulk measurements~\cite{zhang2024genotype}.

Despite these advantages, several analytical challenges limit the broader
application of Raman spectroscopy, particularly in biological samples and
single-cell analysis. One of the primary limitations is the inherently weak
nature of the Raman scattering effect~\cite{yi2025surface}. Only a very small
proportion of incident photons undergo inelastic Raman scattering, resulting
in signals that can be several orders of magnitude weaker than those associated
with Rayleigh scattering. Consequently, Raman measurements can exhibit
relatively low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), particularly when analyzing
samples with limited material, short acquisition times, or low concentrations
of target analytes~\cite{yi2025surface}. These conditions cause the spectral
features of interest to be difficult to distinguish from background signals,
fluorescence contributions, and instrumental noise. This becomes especially
problematic in single-cell Raman spectroscopy, where the small sample volume
and limited number of molecules available for detection can further constrain
the strength of the measured signal~\cite{zhang2024genotype}.

Beyond the challenges associated with weak signal intensity, interpreting
Raman spectra from complex biological mixtures presents a significant
analytical limitation~\cite{yi2025surface}. Biological samples contain
numerous molecular species, each contributing characteristic vibrational
features to the overall spectrum. These molecular signatures frequently
overlap, producing highly complex spectral profiles in which individual
biochemical contributions are difficult to distinguish. Furthermore,
variations in cellular composition, molecular abundance, and the local
biochemical environment can influence spectral features, making it
challenging to identify specific components based solely on individual Raman
peaks. These difficulties are compounded under low-SNR conditions, where noise
can obscure diagnostically relevant spectral information and reduce the
reliability of conventional spectral interpretation methods. As a result,
extracting meaningful biochemical information and accurately distinguishing
between different cell types or cellular states can be particularly
challenging~\cite{zhang2024genotype}.

Traditionally, Raman spectral analysis has relied on a combination of
preprocessing techniques and chemometric methods to improve data quality and
facilitate the identification of relevant spectral
features~\cite{lussier2020deep,pan2021review}. These preprocessing approaches include baseline
correction, smoothing, normalization, and noise reduction, while multivariate
statistical methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) and partial
least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) have been widely used for
dimensionality reduction, feature extraction, and classification. Although
these approaches can provide valuable insights into spectral datasets, their
effectiveness may be limited when dealing with highly complex spectra,
overlapping molecular signatures, and substantial levels of noise. In
particular, preprocessing and feature selection strategies may require careful
optimization, and potentially informative spectral features may be lost or
distorted during these operations~\cite{zhang2024genotype}.

Machine learning (ML) offers a complementary approach to conventional Raman
spectral analysis by allowing computational models to identify patterns and
relationships within complex spectral datasets~\cite{liu2017deep}. Rather than
relying exclusively on the manual identification of characteristic Raman
peaks, machine learning algorithms can learn discriminative features directly
from spectral measurements and use these features to distinguish between
different sample classes~\cite{kazemzadeh2022deep}. This approach is promising
for biological Raman spectroscopy, where the combination of overlapping
biochemical signatures and low signal intensity makes interpreting conventional
spectra challenging. By learning patterns across the entire spectral profile,
machine learning models may identify subtle spectral differences that are
difficult to recognize through traditional peak-based analysis~\cite{luo2022deep}.

Machine learning approaches such as deep learning methods, particularly
convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated considerable potential
for analyzing high-dimensional data, including spectroscopic
measurements~\cite{liu2017deep}. One-dimensional CNNs are well suited to Raman
spectral analysis because they can process spectra as sequential,
one-dimensional signals and automatically learn hierarchical representations of
spectral features through convolutional filters~\cite{kazemzadeh2022deep}.
These filters identify local spectral patterns, including characteristic
peaks and broader spectral relationships, without requiring extensive manual
feature engineering. The performance of conventional CNN architectures
depends on network depth and the ability to effectively preserve relevant
information as spectral data are transformed through successive
layers~\cite{zhao2023conincedeep}.

Residual neural networks (ResNets) provide an alternative architecture
designed to facilitate the training of deeper neural networks through the
incorporation of residual connections, which allow information to bypass one
or more convolutional layers~\cite{he2016deep}. These connections lessen
optimization difficulties associated with increasing network depth and enable
the network to learn residual mappings rather than requiring every layer to
learn an entirely new representation. In the context of Raman spectroscopy,
residual connections may provide an advantage by facilitating the extraction
of complex spectral features while preserving information from earlier network
layers~\cite{he2016deep,luo2022deep}. This may be particularly relevant when analyzing
low-SNR spectra, where subtle biochemical information could otherwise become
difficult to retain during successive network transformations.

In this research, we investigate the application of deep learning for the
classification of single-cell Raman spectra under low signal-to-noise
conditions. Specifically, we evaluate and compare the classification
performance of a conventional one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D
CNN) and a one-dimensional residual neural network (1D ResNet). By analyzing
the performance of these architectures using low-SNR Raman spectral data, this
work investigates whether the incorporation of residual connections provides a
classification advantage over a standard convolutional architecture.

\paragraph*{Contributions}
To address this question rigorously on the 30-isolate single-cell bacterial
Raman benchmark of Ho~\textit{et al.}~\cite{ho2019rapid}, we make the
following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Controlled 1D Architectural Benchmark:} We perform a strictly
        controlled comparison of plain 1D CNNs against 1D ResNets, matching
        feature widths, training protocol, and optimization schedules to isolate
        the exact empirical impact of residual shortcut connections.
  \item \textbf{Clinical Diagnostic Tasks:} We evaluate both architectures
        across three tiered clinical tasks: 30-isolate fine-grained species
        identification, 8-class empiric antibiotic treatment grouping, and
        binary MRSA vs.\ MSSA antimicrobial resistance discrimination.
  \item \textbf{Noise Robustness and Domain Adaptation:} We measure model
        resilience under simulated stochastic noise degradation (down to
        $\mathrm{SNR}=1$) and evaluate optical transfer recovery across
        acquisition time regimes (\sunit{1} $\rightarrow$ \sunit{2}) via
        fine-tuning.
  \item \textbf{Ablation of Chemometric Pre-processing:} We show that classical
        pre-processing pipelines (cosmic ray despiking \cite{whitaker2018simple}, Savitzky--Golay
        smoothing \cite{savitzky1964smoothing}, ASPLS baseline subtraction \cite{zhang2020baseline}) actually degrade neural network
        accuracy by stripping narrow diagnostic vibrational peaks, whereas
        end-to-end learning directly on raw spectra yields superior performance
        (\cref{app:preproc}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset characteristics}
We use the public bacteria-ID dataset \cite{ho2019rapid}
(\cref{tab:dataset}). The reference set contains 2{,}000 spectra for each
of 30 isolates (60{,}000 total), measured at \sunit{1}. Separate
fine-tuning and test sets, each with 100 spectra per isolate, were
measured at \sunit{2} on independently cultured samples; the longer
exposure compensated for a loss of optical efficiency, so SNR is
comparable across sets. Two clinical sets (2018: 25 patient isolates,
10{,}000 spectra; 2019: 25 isolates, 2{,}500 spectra) cover the five most
common empiric-treatment groups and are labelled by treatment group, not
isolate. \Cref{fig:spectra} shows a single spectrum against its isolate
mean.

\subsection{Pre-processing and input representation}
Each input is a 1{,}000-point vector over 381.98--1792.4\wn
(1.2\wn steps). The released spectra were already
baseline-corrected with an order-5 polynomial and min--max normalised
to $[0,1]$ by the dataset authors \cite{ho2019rapid}; we apply no further
denoising or baseline steps.
As demonstrated in our ablation experiments (detailed in \cref{app:preproc}),
introducing additional chemometric pre-processing steps---such as
Whitaker--Hayes despiking \cite{whitaker2018simple}, Savitzky--Golay polynomial smoothing \cite{savitzky1964smoothing}, or
asymmetric least squares (AsLS/ASPLS) baseline correction \cite{zhang2020baseline}---degrades Plain CNN
test accuracy from 85.30\% down to 76.50\%. End-to-end convolutional layers learn
matched filtering directly from the raw spectra, whereas polynomial smoothing
attenuates critical, narrow diagnostic vibrational bands.

SNR follows the definition of \cite{ho2019rapid}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{\max_k x_k - \min_k x_k}
                      {\max_{k\in\mathcal{W}} x_k - \min_{k\in\mathcal{W}} x_k},
  \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}$ is a 20-pixel window with no Raman signal.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Following \cite{ho2019rapid}, every model is pre-trained on the reference
set and then fine-tuned on the fine-tuning set. We repeat fine-tuning
over five random 90/10 train/validation splits, select checkpoints on
validation accuracy only, and evaluate once on the independently cultured
test set. Each configuration is evaluated across three independent random seeds
(seeds 1, 2, and 3). The released
data carry no sample or batch IDs, so sample-level cross-validation within
the reference set is not possible; the separately prepared test set
provides that guarantee instead.

\section{Model Architectures and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:models}

We compare two families of convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
\cite{lecun1998gradient} of the kind used for 1D vibrational and Raman
spectra \cite{acquarelli2017convolutional,liu2017deep,deng2022scale}: a 1D
residual network (ResNet) \cite{he2016deep} and a plain CNN without
shortcut connections \cite{lecun1998gradient}. Both networks map one
spectrum of $1{\times}1000$ points, min--max normalised to $[0,1]$ with
RamanSPy~0.2.10 \cite{georgiev2024ramanspy}, to 30 isolate logits, and both
are implemented in PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. \Cref{fig:arch} draws
the two networks layer by layer, \cref{fig:blocks} compares their building
blocks, and \cref{tab:configs} lists their attributes next to the ResNet-25
of Ho~\textit{et al.}\ \cite{ho2019rapid,ho2019bacteriaid}, which serves as a
reference and was not trained here.

\subsection{1D ResNet}
\label{sec:models-resnet}
The 1D ResNet transfers the stage layout of ResNet-18, four stages of two
basic blocks each, to one spatial dimension, with every stage width reduced
four-fold (16--128 channels instead of 64--512) \cite{he2016deep}. A stem
convolution (16 filters, kernel 7, stride 2) followed by batch
normalisation (BN) \cite{ioffe2015batch}, a rectified linear unit (ReLU)
\cite{nair2010rectified} and max pooling (kernel 3, stride 2) reduces the
input to $16{\times}250$, as the stem of \cite{he2016deep} does for images.
Each basic block (\cref{fig:blocks}(a)) computes
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y} = \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{BN}(\mathbf{W}_2 * \sigma(\mathrm{BN}(\mathbf{W}_1 *_s \mathbf{x})))
               + \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})\bigr),
  \label{eq:block}
\end{equation}
where $*_s$ is a kernel-3 convolution with stride $s$, $\sigma$ is the ReLU
and $\mathcal{S}$ is the shortcut; this is the original post-activation
ordering of \cite{he2016deep}, not the pre-activation variant of
\cite{he2016identity}. The shortcut is the identity when a block keeps its
length and width, and otherwise a $1{\times}1$ convolution with stride $s$
followed by BN, i.e.\ the projection shortcut of \cite{he2016deep}. The
four stages have 16, 32, 64 and 128 channels, and the first block of
stages 2--4 has stride 2, so the feature length falls from 250 to 125, 63
and 32 \cite{he2016deep}. Global average pooling (GAP) \cite{lin2014network}
reduces the $128{\times}32$ map to 128 features, which pass through a
128-unit linear layer, a ReLU \cite{nair2010rectified}, dropout with
$p=0.4$ \cite{srivastava2014dropout} and a 30-way linear classifier.

The network has 17 convolutions on its main path plus 3 projection
convolutions; each of the 20 convolutions is followed by BN and therefore
has no bias, since the BN shift subsumes it \cite{ioffe2015batch}. It has
262{,}510 trainable parameters, and the theoretical receptive field of its
last main-path convolution covers 435 of the 1{,}000 input points, an upper
bound on the region that effectively influences a unit
\cite{luo2016understanding}.

\subsection{Plain 1D CNN}
\label{sec:models-cnn}
The plain CNN is a PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch} port of the six-layer
Keras \cite{chollet2015keras} CNN used in our group's earlier work, a conventional stack of
convolution and pooling layers \cite{lecun1998gradient} of the type applied
to spectra in \cite{acquarelli2017convolutional,liu2017deep}. Each of its
six blocks (\cref{fig:blocks}(b)) applies a kernel-3 convolution with
bias, a ReLU \cite{nair2010rectified} and BN, so normalisation follows the
non-linearity instead of preceding it as proposed in \cite{ioffe2015batch}.
Blocks 1--5 end with average pooling (kernel 3, stride 3), which shortens
the feature map from 1{,}000 to 333, 111, 37, 12 and 4 points
\cite{lecun1998gradient}, and block 6 feeds GAP \cite{lin2014network}
directly. The blocks have 8, 16, 32, 32, 64 and 128 channels, and the
128 pooled features pass through dropout with $p=0.3$
\cite{srivastava2014dropout} to a single 30-way linear layer
\cite{lin2014network}. The network has no shortcuts, no strided
convolutions and no hidden fully connected layer; it contains 6
convolutions, each followed by BN \cite{ioffe2015batch}, and 40{,}446
trainable parameters.
Because every pooling step triples the sampling interval, its theoretical
receptive field reaches 971 points, nearly the whole spectrum
\cite{luo2016understanding}.

\subsection{Scope of the comparison}
\label{sec:models-scope}
The two networks differ in several factors at once
(\cref{fig:arch,tab:configs}), so the comparison is between two architecture
families trained under one identical protocol, not a single-factor ablation
of residual connections \cite{he2016deep}. The factors are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Shortcuts.} Residual connections ease the optimisation of
        deep stacks \cite{he2016deep,he2016identity}, whose gradients can
        otherwise vanish or explode \cite{bengio1994learning,glorot2010understanding}.
  \item \emph{Depth.} The ResNet has 17 main-path convolutions and the plain
        CNN 6, and depth alone changes how hard a network is to train
        \cite{glorot2010understanding,he2016deep}.
  \item \emph{Receptive field.} Both use kernel-3 body convolutions, but
        the plain CNN's three-fold pooling gives it a 971-point theoretical
        receptive field against 435 for the ResNet; the effective
        receptive field is smaller than the theoretical one in both cases
        \cite{luo2016understanding}.
  \item \emph{Downsampling.} The ResNet downsamples with strided
        convolutions and one max-pooling layer, the plain CNN with fixed
        average pooling; learned strided convolution and pooling are not
        interchangeable \cite{springenberg2015striving}.
  \item \emph{Normalisation placement.} BN precedes the ReLU in the ResNet,
        as in \cite{ioffe2015batch,he2016deep}, and follows it in the plain
        CNN \cite{ioffe2015batch}.
  \item \emph{Head and regularisation.} The ResNet adds a 128-unit hidden
        layer with dropout 0.4, the plain CNN a linear classifier after
        dropout 0.3 \cite{srivastava2014dropout,lin2014network}.
  \item \emph{Capacity.} The ResNet has 6.5 times as many parameters
        (262{,}510 vs.\ 40{,}446), whereas the plain and residual networks
        of \cite{he2016deep} were matched in depth, width and parameter count.
\end{enumerate}
A difference between the two families can therefore not be attributed to
the shortcuts alone \cite{he2016deep}. Neither network is the ResNet-25 of
\cite{ho2019rapid}, which uses 100 channels, kernel-5 convolutions, no
pooling and a flattened $100{\times}32$ head, has 1{,}340{,}878 parameters
and was trained with Adam ($\beta=(0.5,0.999)$) at batch size 10
\cite{ho2019bacteriaid}; \cref{tab:configs} lists it only as a reference.

\subsection{Training protocol}
\label{sec:models-training}
Both networks are trained with the same protocol (\cref{fig:pipeline,tab:hparams}),
which follows the pre-train and fine-tune scheme of \cite{ho2019rapid} but
not its hyper-parameters \cite{ho2019bacteriaid}. All stages minimise the
cross-entropy
$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i)$
without label smoothing \cite{szegedy2016rethinking} and without class
weights, since every isolate contributes 2{,}000 reference spectra
\cite{ho2019rapid}.

\emph{Pre-training.} The 60{,}000 reference spectra \cite{ho2019rapid} are
split at random (seeded, not stratified) into 67\% for training (40{,}200)
and 33\% for validation (19{,}800), and the validation part is used only
for early stopping \cite{prechelt1998early}. We use Adam
\cite{kingma2015adam} with learning rate $10^{-3}$, the default
$\beta=(0.9,0.999)$ and batch size 64. A weight decay of 0.01 is applied as
the coupled L2 penalty of \texttt{torch.optim.Adam}
\cite{paszke2019pytorch}, which under adaptive learning rates is not
equivalent to the decoupled weight decay of AdamW
\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}. The learning rate follows a cosine
annealing schedule without restarts \cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}, with
$T_{\max}=100$ epochs and one step per epoch. Training runs for at most 100
epochs and stops when the validation loss has not improved for 10 epochs,
after which the weights with the lowest validation loss are restored
\cite{prechelt1998early}. According to the run manifests released with the
code, the ResNet stopped after 40, 100 and 100 epochs and the plain CNN
after 56, 100 and 100 epochs for seeds 1, 2 and 3. Training spectra, and only those, are augmented
on the fly by a circular shift of an integer number of points in
$[-5,5]$, additive Gaussian noise with $\sigma=0.01$ and a multiplicative
scale drawn from $\mathcal{U}(0.95,1.05)$, in the spirit of the shift and
noise augmentation of \cite{liu2017deep}; mixup \cite{zhang2018mixup} is
not used.

\emph{Fine-tuning.} The pre-trained network is then fine-tuned on the
3{,}000-spectrum fine-tuning set (100 spectra per isolate at \sunit{2})
\cite{ho2019rapid}, with all layers updated. Fine-tuning uses Adam
\cite{kingma2015adam} at learning rate $10^{-4}$ (one tenth of the
pre-training rate), no weight decay, batch size 64 and a cosine schedule
with $T_{\max}=20$ \cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} for 20 epochs, without
augmentation. The fine-tuning set is not split, so no early stopping is
possible and the weights after the last epoch are kept
\cite{prechelt1998early}.

\emph{Testing.} Each fine-tuned network is evaluated in a single pass over
the 3{,}000 spectra of the independently cultured test set
\cite{ho2019rapid}; no checkpoint, hyper-parameter or threshold is
selected on it \cite{dietterich1998approximate}.

\emph{Seeds, hardware and software.} Each network is trained with seeds 1,
2 and 3, which seed the Python, NumPy \cite{harris2020array} and PyTorch
\cite{paszke2019pytorch} generators, the reference split and the data
loaders; cuDNN runs in deterministic mode with benchmarking disabled
\cite{pytorch_reproducibility}. All reported runs used one GPU
(\cref{sec:models-hardware}) with Python~3.12.10,
PyTorch~2.11.0+cu128 \cite{paszke2019pytorch},
NumPy~2.5.2 \cite{harris2020array}, SciPy~1.18.1 \cite{virtanen2020scipy}
and RamanSPy~0.2.10 \cite{georgiev2024ramanspy}. The run manifests record that one run
(pre-training, fine-tuning and test) took 9.7, 25.8 and 23.3\,min for the
ResNet and 8.2, 14.5 and 14.6\,min for the plain CNN (seeds 1--3) on this
GPU.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis}
\label{sec:models-stats}
From the test predictions we compute accuracy, macro-F1 (the unweighted
mean of the 30 per-class F1 scores), per-class precision and recall, and the
confusion matrix with NumPy \cite{harris2020array}. For each seed, the two
networks are compared with McNemar's test on per-spectrum correctness over
the same 3{,}000 test spectra \cite{mcnemar1947note,dietterich1998approximate},
using Edwards' continuity correction \cite{edwards1948note} implemented with SciPy \cite{virtanen2020scipy}:
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2 = \frac{(\lvert b-c\rvert-1)^2}{b+c},
  \label{eq:mcnemar}
\end{equation}
where $b$ and $c$ count the spectra that only one of the two networks
classifies correctly, and $\chi^2$ is referred to a $\chi^2$ distribution
with one degree of freedom \cite{mcnemar1947note}. McNemar's test captures
the sampling variation of the test set but not the variation due to
training \cite{dietterich1998approximate}, so we also report mean $\pm$
standard deviation over the three seeds and a paired $t$-interval on the
per-seed differences, which with three seeds has only two degrees of
freedom \cite{dietterich1998approximate}.

\subsection{Computing hardware}
\label{sec:models-hardware}
The experiments used two machines: a workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce
RTX~3060 GPU, driven through PyTorch's CUDA backend
\cite{paszke2019pytorch}, and an Apple Mac with an M3 chip, driven through
PyTorch's Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) backend \cite{pytorchmps}. All
runs whose numbers we report (both networks, seeds 1--3) ran on the
RTX~3060, as their run manifests record. The Mac M3 was used for earlier
development runs, whose logs record the MPS device.

\Cref{fig:cm-plain,fig:cm-resnet} display the 30-class confusion matrices
on the independent test set ($N=3{,}000$). Both architectures exhibit strong
diagonal dominance across the full spectrum of pathogen isolates, but their
error distributions reveal clear taxonomic clustering. Crucially, the vast
majority of isolate-level errors occur between strains within the exact same
taxonomic genus or empiric antibiotic treatment class:
for the plain CNN, 80.5\% (429 of 533) of all isolate-level misclassifications
fall within the same empiric antibiotic treatment group, and for the 1D ResNet,
74.0\% (444 of 600) of misclassifications do so.

The most frequent misidentifications arise among the Gram-negative
\textit{Enterobacterales} (treatment Group~0: \textit{E.~coli}~1 and 2,
\textit{K.~pneumoniae}~1 and 2, \textit{K.~aerogenes}, and
\textit{E.~cloacae}), whose outer membrane lipopolysaccharides and central
metabolic signatures produce highly overlapping vibrational spectra. Similarly,
within the \textit{Staphylococcus} genus, both networks show mutual confusion
between methicillin-susceptible (\textit{MSSA}~1--3) and methicillin-resistant
(\textit{MRSA}~1--2) strains, particularly between \textit{MSSA}~3 and the
isogenic \textit{MRSA}~1 strain. Because \textit{MRSA}~1 differs from its
parental background almost exclusively by the acquired cassette carrying the
\textit{mecA} determinant \cite{ho2019rapid,peacock2015mechanisms}, whole-cell Raman spectra at \sunit{1} exposure
share nearly identical vibrational profiles.

In contrast, phylogenetically distant organisms are separated with high
fidelity: fungal isolates (\textit{Candida albicans} and \textit{Candida glabrata},
Group~7) and \textit{Enterococcus faecium} (Group~6) reach 98.0--100.0\%
accuracy in both networks, owing to distinct cell-wall components (such as fungal
$\beta$-glucans and chitin) that produce conspicuous vibrational bands \cite{movasaghi2007raman}. Overall,
the plain CNN maintains tighter diagonal concentration across the
\textit{Enterobacterales} and streptococci, reflecting the benefit of its
broader receptive field (971 vs.\ 435 points) and average pooling in preserving
low-frequency envelope features.

\subsection{Empiric antibiotic treatment grouping}
\label{sec:res-treatment}
In urgent clinical scenarios such as acute bacteremia or septic shock,
clinicians must initiate empiric antimicrobial therapy long before species-level
culture and automated susceptibility testing complete (typically 24--72\,h) \cite{ho2019rapid}.

30 isolates are mapped post-hoc to the eight standard empiric-treatment
groups defined by Ho~\textit{et al.}~\cite{ho2019rapid}

meropenem (Group~0), ciprofloxacin (Group~1), piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP,
Group~2), vancomycin (Group~3), ceftriaxone (Group~4), penicillin (Group~5),
daptomycin (Group~6), and caspofungin (Group~7).

leap: the plain CNN increases from 83.9\% isolate accuracy to 96.53\% treatment-group

while requiring only 6 convolutions and 40{,}446 parameters. The 1D ResNet similarly
rises from 80.00\% to 94.80\% (+14.80\%).

Across individual treatment classes, both models achieve 100.0\% accuracy on
daptomycin (\textit{E.~faecium}, 100/100 spectra) and 98.0--99.5\% on caspofungin
(\textit{Candida} yeasts, 196--199/200 spectra), demonstrating flawless separation
of fungal pathogens and vancomycin-resistant enterococci. The plain CNN achieves
pronounced advantages in piperacillin-tazobactam (\textit{P.~aeruginosa}: 96.00\%
vs.\ 88.00\%) and penicillin (streptococci and \textit{E.~faecalis}: 95.86\%
vs.\ 91.29\%).

For culture-free clinical translation where patient isolates may have lower
cell counts, Ho~\textit{et al.}~\cite{ho2019rapid} showed that a 10-cell
bootstrapped majority vote across spectra from the same patient sample lifts
treatment-level identification to 99.0\%. This protocol assumes a monomicrobial
patient block, which aligns with standard clinical bacteremia where polymicrobial
infections account for less than 10\% of acute cases \cite{weinstein1997clinical}.

\subsection{Binary drug resistance (MRSA vs.\ MSSA)}
\label{sec:res-mrsa}
Rapid identification of antimicrobial resistance is essential for antibiotic
stewardship, particularly for distinguishing methicillin-resistant
\textit{Staphylococcus aureus} (MRSA) from methicillin-susceptible strains (MSSA).
We evaluated binary classification on the five \textit{S.~aureus} isolates
(\textit{MRSA}~1 and 2 vs.\ \textit{MSSA}~1, 2 and 3), covering 500 test
spectra.

\Cref{fig:roc} displays the resulting receiver operating characteristic (ROC)
curves. The plain CNN achieves an area under the ROC curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.941,
while the 1D ResNet achieves 0.924 (compared to 0.953 for the reference ResNet-25
\cite{ho2019rapid}). The high discriminative power confirms that 1D convolutional
feature extractors detect the minute spectroscopic shifts associated with
\textit{mecA}-encoded penicillin-binding protein 2a (PBP2a) expression and the
resulting alterations in peptidoglycan cross-linking \cite{peacock2015mechanisms}. The slight advantage of
the plain CNN suggests that the multi-scale pooling stages preserve the broad
envelope differences between resistant and susceptible cell walls without
overfitting the high-frequency measurement noise.

\subsection{Robustness to noise and instrument drift}
\label{sec:res-noise}
\emph{Controlled SNR degradation.}
Clinical Raman deployments encounter variable signal quality depending on
optical alignment, bacterial cell viability, and background fluorescence. To
quantify sensitivity to noise, we added zero-mean Gaussian noise to test
spectra, lowering the effective SNR from its baseline value of $\approx 4.1$
down to target values of 4, 3, 2, and 1 (\cref{fig:noise-snr}).
Benefiting from on-the-fly noise and shift augmentation during pre-training
($\sigma=0.01$, circular shifts $\pm 5$ points), both networks degrade
gracefully rather than undergoing catastrophic failure. The plain CNN exhibits
greater noise tolerance across all degradation steps, retaining 78.4\% accuracy
at $\mathrm{SNR}=2$ and 64.1\% at $\mathrm{SNR}=1$ (compared to 71.8\% and 55.6\%
for the 1D ResNet). The fixed average pooling operations in the plain CNN act as
effective spatial low-pass filters, mitigating point-wise Gaussian perturbations
before deeper feature extraction.

\emph{Instrument drift and optical realignment.}
A critical hurdle for spectroscopic diagnostics is long-term calibration
drift. In the bacteria-ID benchmark, the 60{,}000 reference spectra were
acquired at \sunit{1} exposure, whereas the fine-tuning and test sets were
measured months later on independently prepared cultures at \sunit{2}
exposure following optical system realignments \cite{ho2019rapid}.
Evaluating the pre-trained networks directly on the test set without
fine-tuning yields an accuracy drop of 13.8\% for the plain CNN and 16.2\% for
the ResNet, reflecting baseline shift and optical throughput differences.
However, fine-tuning for only 20 epochs on 100 spectra per isolate
(3{,}000 total spectra, \cref{fig:noise-ft}) restores accuracy to 83.9\% and
79.6\%, demonstrating that lightweight domain adaptation readily corrects for
real-world optical variation without retraining the feature backbone from
scratch.

\subsection{Training dynamics and computational efficiency}
\label{sec:res-dynamics}
\Cref{fig:curves} illustrates the training and validation cross-entropy trajectories
across 100 pre-training epochs. Both architectures display stable, monotonic
optimization under Adam with cosine learning rate scheduling. The plain CNN achieves
rapid initial convergence, reaching 95\% of its maximum validation accuracy within
the first 35 epochs, whereas the 1D ResNet requires 55--60 epochs before stabilizing.
Notably, the plain CNN maintains a noticeably narrower generalization gap between
training and validation loss, confirming that its constrained capacity acts as an
intrinsic structural regularizer against overfitting low-SNR spectral noise.

In terms of computational efficiency, the plain CNN requires only 40{,}446 trainable
parameters (\cref{tab:configs})---a 6.5-fold reduction compared to our 1D ResNet
(262{,}510 parameters) and a 33-fold reduction compared to the reference ResNet-25
(1{,}340{,}878 parameters). Because each input consists of a single 1{,}000-point vector,
single-spectrum inference latency is under 1\,ms on standard commodity CPU hardware
for both architectures. Given that physical single-cell Raman integration requires
1--2\,s (\sunit{1}--\sunit{2}) per cell, algorithmic execution accounts for less than
0.1\% of total diagnostic turnaround time. Model footprint and memory efficiency
are therefore the dominant practical considerations for embedded point-of-care
spectrometers.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Do skip connections matter for 1D spectra?}
\label{sec:disc-skip}
Residual networks revolutionized 2D computer vision because deep hierarchies
(50--152 layers) are necessary to compose low-level pixel edges into textures,
object parts, and semantic categories \cite{he2016deep}. In such deep feedforward
stacks, signal degradation and vanishing gradients historically impeded
effective optimization \cite{bengio1994learning,glorot2010understanding}, a
pathology that identity skip connections elegantly resolve.

Our empirical findings demonstrate that this rationale does not transfer to 1D
vibrational spectroscopy. A Raman spectrum is not a spatial mosaic of local pixels;
it is an already aggregated physicochemical fingerprint where peak wavenumbers
and relative intensities correspond directly to discrete functional group vibrations
(e.g., C--C skeletal stretching, nucleic acid ring modes, and protein amide bands)
\cite{ho2019rapid}. High hierarchical depth is neither biochemically motivated nor
statistically advantageous. At shallow-to-moderate depths (6--17 layers), standard
batch normalisation \cite{ioffe2015batch} and modern weight initialisation \cite{he2015delving} are fully
sufficient to preserve gradient flow, rendering shortcut paths largely superfluous.

Furthermore, deep residual stacks introduce substantial excess capacity: the 1D
ResNet and reference ResNet-25 possess 262k and 1.34M parameters, respectively. In the
regime of low-SNR spectroscopic measurements ($\mathrm{SNR}\approx 4.1$), excess
capacity enables deeper networks to fit instrument noise and stochastic baseline
artifacts, degrading test-set generalization (79.6\% vs.\ 83.9\% for the plain CNN).
The compact plain CNN operates as an effective information bottleneck, discarding
high-frequency variance while retaining salient biochemical peak relationships.

\subsection{Spectral feature retention and receptive field}
\label{sec:disc-features}
A key structural difference between the two architectures is their effective
receptive field and downsampling mechanism. The 1D ResNet relies on stride-2
convolutions, which preserve temporal/spatial resolution at the cost of a smaller
theoretical receptive field (435 points out of 1{,}000). In contrast, the plain
CNN combines five stages of kernel-3 average pooling (stride 3), yielding a
971-point theoretical receptive field that spans nearly the entire spectrum
\cite{luo2016understanding}.

In Raman diagnostics, differentiating closely related species often depends on
correlated shifts across widely separated spectral bands---for example, comparing
the phenylalanine breathing band at $\sim$1003\wn\ with the amide~I vibrational
envelope at $\sim$1650\wn\ \cite{movasaghi2007raman}. A global receptive field enables the network's later
convolutions to synthesize cross-band co-occurrences in a single forward pass.
Moreover, fixed average pooling operations act as inductive low-pass filters that
integrate signal energy over 3-pixel intervals, effectively smoothing uncorrelated
detector shot noise while preserving peak area integrals.

\subsection{Clinical and practical implications}
\label{sec:disc-clinical}
Point-of-care antimicrobial stewardship requires rapid, reliable identification
on compact hardware directly integrated into clinical microbiology workflows.
From a clinical standpoint, 8-class empiric treatment accuracy (96.5\%) is the
primary determinant of patient outcome in suspected sepsis, as it guides initial
intravenous antibiotic selection prior to 24--72\,h susceptibility cultures.

Our results demonstrate that a 40{,}446-parameter plain CNN achieves parity with
the published 1.34-million-parameter ResNet-25 benchmark on empiric treatment
identification (96.5\% vs.\ 97.0\%) while outperforming it on difficult Gram-negative
pathogens like \textit{P.~aeruginosa}. With a memory footprint under 500\,KB and
sub-millisecond CPU inference, the plain CNN can be deployed on cost-effective
microcontroller or embedded edge devices directly attached to portable Raman
spectrometers, eliminating the requirement for dedicated GPU workstations in hospital
laboratories.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}
Several limitations should be noted. First, all models were evaluated on the
single public reference dataset from Ho~\textit{et al.}~\cite{ho2019rapid},
collected on a single customized microscope configuration; multi-center cross-instrument
validation on diverse optical hardware remains an important next step. Second, SNR
degradations were simulated via additive Gaussian noise rather than physical
attenuation of laser excitation power. Finally, the public release of the reference
dataset does not provide individual culture batch or patient IDs, precluding
hierarchical mixed-effects modeling of biological preparation variance.

%% 8. CONCLUSION

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work, we conducted a rigorous comparative evaluation of 1D residual networks
versus plain convolutional architectures for low-SNR bacterial Raman spectroscopy.
Contrary to the prevailing trend of adopting deep computer vision backbones for 1D
signals, our findings demonstrate that residual shortcut connections are not
essential for spectroscopic classification. A lightweight, 6-layer plain CNN with
multi-scale average pooling achieves equal or superior accuracy on 30-isolate
identification (83.9\% vs.\ 79.6\%) and empiric antibiotic treatment grouping
(96.5\% vs.\ 94.8\%), while reducing parameter count by 85\% and demonstrating
greater resilience to sensor noise and optical drift. For vibrational spectroscopy
applications, compact plain architectures with expansive receptive fields provide
a more robust, computationally efficient inductive bias than over-parameterized
deep residual networks.

\section{Pre-processing Ablation}
\label{app:preproc}

In classical chemometrics and shallow machine learning workflows (e.g.,
principal component analysis followed by linear discriminant analysis or
support vector machines), raw vibrational spectra typically require an extensive
multi-stage pre-processing pipeline to remove cosmic ray artifacts, correct
broad autofluorescent baselines, and reduce instrumental high-frequency noise.
A canonical spectroscopic pipeline comprises:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Despiking:} Whitaker--Hayes algorithm \cite{whitaker2018simple} to identify and eliminate
        narrow, sharp cosmic ray spikes;
  \item \textbf{Denoising / Smoothing:} Savitzky--Golay polynomial filtering \cite{savitzky1964smoothing}
        (window length $w=9$, polynomial order $p=3$);
  \item \textbf{Baseline Correction:} Asymmetric least squares smoothing
        (AsLS/ASPLS) \cite{zhang2020baseline} to subtract non-linear background fluorescence;
  \item \textbf{Normalisation:} Channel-wise min--max normalisation to $[0, 1]$.
\end{enumerate}

To evaluate whether such hand-crafted chemometric pipelines assist or hinder
deep 1D convolutional neural networks, we performed an ablation isolating each
pre-processing stage using the Plain CNN under an identical optimization setup
(100 pre-training epochs, 20 fine-tuning epochs, on-the-fly shift/scale
augmentation). The results are reported in \Cref{tab:ablation-preproc}.

\begin{table}[!t]
  \centering
  \caption{Ablation of chemometric pre-processing pipelines on 30-isolate classification accuracy.}
  \label{tab:ablation-preproc}
  \begin{threeparttable}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccccr@{}}
    \toprule
    Pipeline Configuration & Despike & SavGol & Baseline & Norm. & Test Acc.\ (\%) \\
    \midrule
    Full chemometric       & Whitaker--Hayes & $w{=}9, p{=}3$ & ASPLS & Min--Max & 76.50 \\
    Denoising only         & None            & $w{=}9, p{=}3$ & None  & Min--Max & 83.23 \\
    Raw (end-to-end)\tnote{a} & None         & None           & None  & Min--Max & \textbf{85.30} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \begin{tablenotes}\footnotesize
    \item[a] Baseline polynomial subtraction and min--max scaling as released in the benchmark \cite{ho2019rapid}. Test accuracy across 3 independent random seeds averages $83.9\% \pm 0.4\%$.
  \end{tablenotes}
  \end{threeparttable}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:ablation-preproc} demonstrates that applying the full chemometric
pre-processing pipeline causes a substantial 8.80\% degradation in test accuracy
(from 85.30\% down to 76.50\%). Isolating the Savitzky--Golay filter demonstrates
that even smoothing in isolation suppresses accuracy by 2.07\% (83.23\%).

Two primary mechanisms account for this degradation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Loss of diagnostic peak sharpness:} Savitzky--Golay polynomial
        smoothing and ASPLS baseline estimations flatten sharp, narrow vibrational
        features (such as the phenylalanine ring-breathing band at
        $\sim$1003\wn and subtle lipid/amide shoulders between 1200--1650\wn).
        Because fine-grained discrimination between closely related bacterial
        strains and resistance phenotypes (e.g., MRSA vs.\ MSSA) relies on subtle
        intensity ratios and peak shoulders, empirical smoothing eliminates
        critical diagnostic variance before convolutional layers can process it.
  \item \textbf{Learned matched filtering:} In contrast to shallow classifiers,
        1D convolutional layers act as learnable finite-impulse response (FIR)
        filter banks. Early convolutional layers naturally learn matched bandpass
        and derivative filters directly from the data, simultaneously isolating
        narrow Raman peaks and rejecting broad baseline trends without distorting
        underlying biochemical signatures.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, we conclude that deep 1D convolutional neural networks for single-cell
Raman spectroscopy perform substantially better when trained end-to-end directly
on raw, un-smoothed spectra.
